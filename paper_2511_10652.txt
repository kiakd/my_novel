Cognitively-Inspired Episodic Memory Architectures for Accurate and 
Efficient Character AI 
 
Rafael Arias Gonzalez   and   Steve DiPaola 
Simon Fraser University 
sdipaola@sfu.ca 
 
 
Large language models show promise for embodying historical characters in dialogue systems, but existing approaches face a critical trade-
off: simple retrieval-augmented generation produces shallow responses, while multi -stage reflection achieves depth at prohibitive latency. 
We present an architecture that resolves this tension through offline data augmentation and efficient parallel retrieval from structured episodic 
memory. Our system transforms biographical data into 1,774 enriched first-person memories with affective-semantic metadata, then employs 
two-stage retrieval achieving 0.52s prompt generation. Evaluation using LLM -as-judge and RAGAs metrics shows our approach achieves 
parity with traditional RAG on GPT-4 while significantly outperforming it on smaller models (GPT-3.5, GPT-3), suggesting particular value 
for resource-constrained deployments. Beyond dialogue, the structured memory enables novel visualization tools: spatiotemporal heatmaps, 
emotional trajectory analysis, and interactive path tracki ng, positioning the system as both a dialogue interface and research tool for 
biographical analysis. We use Van Gogh as a test case, but the architecture is generalizable to any historical figure with su bstantial textual 
records, offering a practical framework for educational, museum, and research applications requiring both accuracy and efficiency.

1 INTRODUCTION 
Creating believable AI character agents has evolved from rule-based systems to contemporary LLM 
implementations, yet fundamental challenges persist. Historical figures present a particularly compelling 
use case. Their lives are well-documented through biographies, letters, and archival materials, yet 
traditional approaches to character embodiment struggle to transform this rich textual heritage into 
coherent, interactive experiences. 
Recent advances in LLMs have renewed interest in character-based dialogue systems. However, when 
deployed without grounding mechanisms, these systems exhibit a persistent tendency toward 
hallucination, generating plausible but factually incorrect information with high confidence. For 
historical figures like Vincent van Gogh, where accuracy matters for educational and cultural 
preservation applications, such fabrications undermine system trustworthiness and limit practical 
deployment. 
  
 2 
Current approaches to character embodiment face a fundamental trade-off between accuracy and 
responsiveness. Simple retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems can ground responses in factual 
data but often produce shallow, disconnected answers that fail to capture the experiential richness of a 
character’s lived history. Multi-stage architectures incorporating self-reflection, iterative refinement, and 
chained retrievals achieve greater depth and coherence but at severe computational cost. Systems 
requiring multiple sequential LLM calls can take tens of seconds to construct a single response, far too 
slow for natural conversation and prohibitively expensive for real-time educational applications. 
Our work addresses a gap in conversational AI: existing systems optimize either for accuracy (through 
expensive multi-stage processing) or efficiency (through simple retrieval), but educational and museum 
deployments require both. By conducting reflection and enrichment offline, we achieve the contextual 
depth of multi-stage systems at single-retrieval latency. This design philosophy—separating concerns 
temporally rather than eliminating them—offers a generalizable approach to resource-constrained AI 
deployment. 
This latency problem becomes particularly acute when considering deployment constraints. While large-
scale models like GPT-4 and Claude v4 with large context windows offer impressive capabilities, 
educational institutions, museums, and research environments often require systems that can run locally 
or on modest cloud infrastructure. Smaller models that enable such deployment show even greater 
performance degradation with traditional RAG approaches, making the efficiency-accuracy gap a 
critical barrier to practical application. 
We present an architecture that resolves the efficiency-accuracy dilemma through a novel combination 
of offline data augmentation and real-time parallel retrieval. Our key insight is that the computationally 
expensive work of self-reflection, context enrichment, and perspective transformation can be performed 
once during dataset construction rather than repeatedly during each interaction. By pre-processing 
biographical and epistolary data into structured episodic memories enriched with affective-semantic 
metadata (temporal markers, emotional valence/arousal, character relationships, geographic locations), 
we create a substrate that enables both rapid retrieval and rich contextualization. 
Our system implements a three-tier memory architecture inspired by cognitive models: 
1. Long-term episodic memory:1,774 first-person experiential memories derived from biographic text 
letters 
2. Intermediate conversational memory: dynamically maintained records of recent dialogue  
3. Short-term working memory (the LLM’s context window). 
A two-stage retrieval mechanism accesses these memory tiers in parallel. Initial similarity search uses 
concise memory descriptions optimized for matching user queries, then retrieves full first-person 
narratives and associated affective-semantic metadata for prompt construction. The result is a system 
achieving an average prompt generation time of 0.52 seconds while maintaining response quality that 
outperforms traditional RAG implementations, particularly for smaller, more deployable models. Our 
evaluation using LLM-as-a-judge and RAGAs (RAG assessment) frameworks demonstrates consistent 
advantages in faithfulness and contextual relevance across multiple model scales. 
While conversational interaction represents the most visible application of our architecture, the 
structured nature of our episodic memory dataset enables a complementary use case: interactive 
visualization and exploration of historical figures’ cognitive and experiential landscapes. Our affective-
semantic enrichment (valence, arousal, geographic coordinates, character co-occurrence, 
autobiographical significance) transforms biographical data into a multidimensional space that can be 
analyzed, visualized, and navigated. We demonstrate applications including spatiotemporal heatmaps, 
  
 3 
emotional trajectory analysis, social network visualization, and user path tracking through memory 
space during conversations. This dual-use positioning distinguishes our work from pure chatbot systems, 
serving both real-time dialogue generation and contemplative historical investigation. We demonstrate 
our approach using Vincent van Gogh, whose extensive documentation provides ideal source material 
but represents a generalizable case.  
We contribute three advances to conversational AI for character embodiment: 
1. An efficient episodic memory architecture achieving sub-second prompt generation (0.52s average) 
through offline augmentation and parallel retrieval, enabling real-time dialogue on resource-constrained 
hardware. 
2. A multi-stage augmentation methodology transforming unstructured biographical materials into 
structured first-person memories with affective-semantic metadata (valence, arousal, geocoding, 
temporal markers). 
3. Empirical evidence that structured episodic memory democratizes character embodiment: while 
achieving parity with traditional RAG on large models (GPT-4), our approach significantly outperforms 
it on smaller models (GPT-3.5, GPT-3), with implications for sustainable and accessible AI deployment. 
2 RELATED WORK 
We build on four research threads: character embodiment, memory architectures, RAG systems, and 
LLM-driven data augmentation. 
Character Embodiment and Role-Playing Systems: Recent work on character embodiment has 
explored various approaches to creating believable AI agents. Park et al.’s (2023) Generative Agents 
demonstrated sophisticated behavioral simulation through memory streams and reflection mechanisms, 
though at substantial computational cost due to continuous LLM invocations. Character-LLM (Shao et 
al., 2023) achieves strong performance through fine-tuning character-specific models on first-person 
narratives, but requires separate training for each character and retraining for knowledge updates. They 
acknowledge that relying primarily on Wikipedia limits biographical depth compared to comprehensive 
sources. RoleLLM (Wang et al., 2023) emphasizes rich character descriptions in prompts and notes that 
explicitly instructing models to reference context directly yields better responses. Li et al.’s (2023) 
ChatHaruhi similarly highlights the importance of personality cues and conversational history retrieval. 
Unlike fine-tuning approaches requiring per-character model training, our retrieval-based architecture 
enables rapid character instantiation through dataset construction alone. Compared to continuous 
reflection systems, we achieve equivalent depth through offline processing, eliminating runtime 
computational overhead. 
Memory Systems and Cognitive Architectures: Memory systems in AI agents draw inspiration from 
cognitive science models distinguishing short-term, working, and long-term memory (Cowan, 2008), 
with Tulving’s (1972) distinction between semantic and episodic memory providing theoretical 
grounding. Recent implementations include Kim et al.’s (2023) memory-augmented LLM 
personalization and Zhang et al.’s (2023) user modeling systems, which focus on learning individual 
preferences rather than instantiating pre-defined historical characters. Sant’Anna et al.’s (2023) work on 
autonoetic consciousness (subjective re-experiencing of past events) has received limited attention in AI 
systems despite its relevance to episodic memory. We explicitly model three memory tiers inspired by 
cognitive architectures while grounding long-term memory in structured episodic representations 
enriched with affective-semantic metadata. 
  
 4 
RAG Architectures: Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) grounds LLM outputs in 
external knowledge. Gao et al.’s (2024) survey categorizes RAG systems from naive single-retrieval to 
sophisticated modular architectures, noting that while modular designs offer adaptability, they incur 
substantial computational cost through multiple LLM invocations. Shuster et al. (2021) found that RAG 
substantially improves factual accuracy in conversational AI, particularly relevant for character 
embodiment requiring biographical fidelity. Recent advances in complex RAG patterns including self-
reflection, tool-interactive critiquing, and iterative self-refinement (Ji et al., 2023; Gou et al., 2024; 
Madaan et al., 2023) enhance output quality but introduce latency incompatible with real-time 
interaction. We achieve RAG-level grounding while approaching naive RAG speed through offline 
preprocessing. By conducting “self-reflection” during dataset construction rather than runtime, we 
obtain the contextual depth of multi-stage systems at single-retrieval latency. 
Efficiency and Model Scaling Considerations: The tension between model capability and deployment 
practicality has received growing attention. Strubell et al. (2019), Desislavov et al. (2021), and 
Thompson et al.(2021) document substantial energy costs and diminishing returns in large model 
development. The emergence of smaller models like Gemma 2B/7B, Llama 3.1 8B, and Mistral 7B 
reflects industry recognition of deployment constraints (Gemma Team et al., 2024; Meta, 2024; Mistral 
AI, 2024). Our design specifically targets deployment on smaller models through strict token budgets 
(2000 tokens total) and optimized retrieval efficiency, anticipating edge deployment scenarios where 
computational resources, energy consumption, and inference costs require careful management. 
LLMs as Data Augmentation Tools: These have gained traction across NLP tasks, from conversational 
reformulations (Chen et al., 2024) to few-shot entity recognition (Ye et al., 2024) and multilingual 
augmentation (Whitehouse et al., 2023). In HCI contexts, LLM-generated synthetic data for user studies 
(Hämäläinen et al., 2023; Miotto et al., 2022) focus primarily on generating new participants rather than 
transforming existing biographical materials. We contribute a multi-stage augmentation pipeline 
specifically designed for biographical data transformation, systematically converting third-person 
narratives to first-person perspectives (autonoetic) while extracting affective-semantic metadata. 
Evaluation Methodologies: Evaluating character-based dialogue systems presents challenges, as 
traditional metrics like BLEU and ROUGE poorly capture emotional authenticity and perspective 
consistency (Gao et al., 2024). Zheng et al.’s (2023) LLM-as-a-judge framework achieves 80%+ 
agreement with human evaluators while identifying key biases (position, self-enhancement, verbosity). 
Es et al.’s (2023) RAGAs framework provides reference-free metrics for RAG systems: Context 
Relevance, Answer Relevance, and Faithfulness. We employ both methodologies complementarily, 
enabling comparative evaluation against baselines while assessing retrieval and grounding mechanisms 
without requiring extensive human annotation. 
 
Across these threads, a gap remains between deep, reflective architectures that are too slow for 
interaction and fast RAG pipelines that lack experiential richness. By performing reflection offline and 
encoding results as structured memories, our approach delivers depth, grounding, and responsiveness in 
a single design. 
3 SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
Our architecture addresses the efficiency-accuracy dilemma through a principled separation of concerns: 
computationally expensive operations occur once during offline dataset construction, while runtime 
operations prioritize speed through parallel retrieval and structured access patterns. This design enables

sub-second prompt generation while maintaining the contextual richness typically associated with multi-
stage reflection systems. Figure 1 provides an overview of the complete system, showing the 
relationship between offline augmentation, runtime retrieval, and dialogue generation.

3.1 Design Philosophy and Generalizability 
The architecture comprises four main stages: 
1. Offline Data Augmentation: Multi-stage LLM processing transforms source materials into 
structured episodic memories enriched with affective-semantic metadata 
2. Memory Indexing: Processed memories are embedded and organized into retrievable indexes 
3. Runtime Retrieval: User queries trigger parallel search across memory tiers with two-stage access 

  
 6 
4. Prompt Construction and Generation: Retrieved memories and metadata populate LLM context 
for response generation 
This separation between offline and online processing proves critical for efficiency. Operations 
requiring iterative LLM calls, information extraction, and data enrichment occur once during dataset 
construction. Runtime operations reduce to embedding similarity search and prompt assembly, both 
computationally inexpensive operations achievable in sub-second timeframes. The system requires only 
two inputs: (1) substantial textual materials about the character (biography, letters, interviews, memoirs), 
and (2) character-defining attributes for static context (personality traits, communication style, historical 
period). 
3.2 Three-Tier Memory Architecture 
Inspired by cognitive models distinguishing memory types by temporal scope and accessibility (Cowan, 
2008), our system implements three memory tiers that parallel human memory organization while 
remaining computationally tractable. 
3.2.1 Long-Term Episodic Memory 
The foundation of our system is a structured dataset of first-person experiential memories derived from 
biographical source materials. Each memory entry contains: 
• Narrative content: First-person account of an experience, including setting, actions, thoughts, and 
emotions 
• Temporal markers: Dates situating the memory in the character’s lifespan 
• Affective metadata: Emotional valence (pleasant/unpleasant dimension) and arousal (activation 
level) 
• Social context: Characters present or mentioned in the memory 
• Geographic data: Locations as geocoded coordinates 
• Relevance scores: Autobiographical significance ratings 
For our Van Gogh implementation, this dataset comprises 1,774 memories spanning 1853-1890, with 
particularly dense coverage during his artistically productive periods (1880-1890). The dataset structure 
(Table 1 in Section 4) generalizes to any historical figure; the fields remain consistent while content 
varies by individual. Long-term memory serves dual purposes: it provides retrievable content for 
dialogue generation and enables analytical visualization of the character’s experiential landscape. 
3.2.2 Intermediate and Short Term Conversational Memory 
While long-term episodic memory remains static (representing the character’s fixed biographical 
history), conversational exchanges generate new experiential data requiring retention for thematic 
continuity. We implement intermediate memory through dynamic accumulation of query-response pairs 
embedded into a secondary retrieval index. 
After each dialogue turn, the system concatenates the user query and LLM response into a single text 
unit, generates an embedding representation, and stores it in a second retrieval index (RAG2) alongside 
the original long-term memory embeddings. Subsequent queries trigger similarity search across both 
long-term biographical memories and recent conversational exchanges. If conversational memory yields 
high-similarity results, the system performs a secondary retrieval using the conversation memory as a 
query against long-term memory, establishing explicit connections between current discussion topics 
and relevant biographical experiences. 
  
 7 
This mechanism enables thematic continuity without expanding context window requirements. 
Intermediate memory exists only within individual conversation sessions. Resetting the chatbot clears 
conversational memory while preserving long-term biographical memory, analogous to starting a new 
interview with the same historical figure. The LLM’s context window serves as short-term working 
memory, holding immediately active information for response generation. Our implementation 
maintains strict token budgets to ensure compatibility with smaller models and future edge deployment 
with a 2000 token total:.  
• Retrieved long-term memories: 600 tokens; 
• Response generation: 500 tokens 
• Static context: 300 tokens 
• Affective-semantic metadata: 100 tokens; 
• Recent conversation history: 500 tokens 
This conservative allocation reflects our target deployment context. While contemporary models support 
large token windows, smaller models suitable for local deployment (Gemma 7B, Llama 3 8B, Mistral 
7B) typically offer 2k-8k contexts. Our 2k budget ensures forward compatibility with emerging edge-
deployable models while enforcing discipline in information selection. 
3.3 Two-Stage Retrieval Mechanism 
A key architectural innovation is our two-stage retrieval process, which separates the representation used 
for similarity matching from the content provided to the LLM for generation. This design resolves a 
fundamental tension: concise descriptions enable better query matching, while rich first-person 
narratives enable expressive response generation. 
Stage One: Similarity-Based Retrieval:  When a user poses a query, the system embeds the query using 
the same embedding model employed for memory indexing (OpenAI’s text-embedding-ada-002), 
computes cosine similarity between the query embedding and all memory embeddings, ranks memories 
by similarity score, and retrieves top-k candidates (k=3 in our implementation). 
The embeddings represent not the full first-person narratives but augmented context descriptions, 
condensed summaries capturing the core event, temporal reference, key characters, and primary 
location. For example: “Argument with brother Theo about financial support and artistic direction, 
December 1881, The Hague. Present: Theo van Gogh (mentioned), parents (mentioned).” This concise 
representation aligns better with typical user queries, which often reference topics, time periods, or 
relationships rather than matching the narrative style of first-person memories. 
Stage Two: Content Retrieval and Enrichment: Having identified relevant memories through similarity 
search, the system retrieves their complete content for prompt construction: first-person narrative, 
temporal markers, affective metadata (valence/arousal values), social context (characters present or 
mentioned), geographic data, and relevance score. This two-stage approach (searching on concise 
descriptions but generating from rich narratives) optimizes both retrieval precision and expressive 
capacity. 
3.4 Parallel RAG Architecture 
Our system employs two Retrieval-Augmented Generation pipelines operating in parallel, both querying 
the same underlying episodic memory dataset but serving different retrieval purposes. RAG1 (Long-
term Memory Access) directly retrieves episodic memories based on query similarity. RAG2 
(Conversational Memory + Associated Episodics) maintains embeddings of query-response pairs from 
  
 8 
the current conversation. When conversational memory retrieval succeeds, RAG2 performs a secondary 
retrieval using the conversation memory as a query against the long-term episodic dataset.  
This parallelization enables efficient thematic continuity without sequential dependency. If RAG2 finds 
relevant conversational memory, it retrieves one associated long-term memory, allowing the final 
prompt to include: 1 conversational memory (showing topical continuity), 1 long-term memory related 
to that conversation, and 1-2 long-term memories directly matching the current query. If no 
conversational memory meets the similarity threshold, the system defaults to retrieving 3 long-term 
memories directly relevant to the current query. Figure 2 shows the complete retrieval process.

3.5 Prompt Construction Strategy 
Retrieved memories and metadata populate the LLM’s context window following a structured template. 
The prompt includes: static character context (300 tokens) with personality traits and role-playing 
instructions; retrieved episodic memories (600 tokens) with first-person narratives, dates, and locations; 
affective-semantic metadata (100 tokens) including emotional state, characters present, and relevance 
scores; and recent conversation history (500 tokens). This structure provides the LLM with multiple 
context layers: stable character definition, specific relevant experiences, emotional and social context, 
and conversational continuity. 
3.6 Efficiency Analysis 
The architecture’s efficiency derives from three design decisions. First, offline complexity, online 
simplicity: expensive operations (perspective transformation, information extraction, emotional 
annotation) occur once during dataset construction, while runtime operations reduce to similarity search 
and prompt assembly. Second, parallelization without sequential dependency: both RAG pipelines query 
simultaneously rather than sequentially, maintaining constant time complexity. Third, pre-computed 
embeddings: by caching embeddings for all long-term memories and reusing them across sessions, we 
eliminate repeated API calls. 
The result is an average prompt generation time of 0.52 seconds measured across hundreds of test 
interactions. At typical GPT-4 generation speeds (~5-7 tokens/second), the model would generate only 
3-4 words in the time our system constructs a complete, contextually rich 1500-token prompt. Multi-

  
 9 
stage reflection systems requiring 3-5 sequential LLM calls would require 15-30+ seconds for 
equivalent complexity, a latency incompatible with conversational interaction expectations. 
Our modular implementation uses OpenAI APIs with support for local models via Hugging Face 
transformers, enabling seamless transition from cloud-based prototyping to local deployment. 
4 MULTI-STAGE DATASET CONSTRUCTION  
This section describes our methodology for transforming unstructured biographical materials into 
structured episodic memory datasets enriched with affective-semantic metadata. While we demonstrate 
the approach using Vincent van Gogh as our exemplar, the pipeline generalizes to any historical figure 
with substantial textual documentation. 
4.1 Source Material Requirements and Selection 
Our approach requires two types of source materials: biographical narratives (third-person accounts of 
the figure’s life) and first-person documents (letters, diaries, memoirs, interviews). The combination 
proves essential since biographies provide chronological structure and contextual framing, while first-
person materials reveal subjective perspective and authentic voice. 
Biography: We used Naifeh and Smith’s Van Gogh: The Life (2011), a comprehensive 976-page 
biography created in collaboration with the Van Gogh Museum and incorporating previously 
unpublished archival materials. This work spans Van Gogh’s complete lifespan (1853-1890) with 
particular depth during his artistic period (1880-1890). The biography was segmented into 
approximately 912 chunks of ~3000 characters each (roughly 3 paragraphs), balancing the need to 
capture contextual richness while representing distinct episodic experiences. 
Letters: Van Gogh’s correspondence comprises 863 letters written both by and to him. These 
documents provide direct access to his thoughts, emotional states, and subjective interpretations of life 
events. However, they present significant processing challenges: fragmented structure, assumed 
contextual knowledge, and lack of explicit temporal markers and location specificity that would enable 
effective retrieval. 
For generalization to other historical figures, our methodology requires: comprehensive third-person 
biographical coverage spanning significant life periods; first-person materials revealing subjective 
perspective (optional but highly beneficial); and texts in machine-readable format. Figures meeting these 
criteria include scientists, political leaders, authors, and artists with documented textual legacies. The 
pipeline relies on general-purpose LLM capabilities applicable across biographical contexts. 
4.2 Multi-Stage Augmentation Pipeline 
Figure 3 illustrates our complete augmentation pipeline, comprising four sequential LLM-driven stages 
followed by automated enrichment and quality assurance.

Figure 3. Multi-Stage Primary and Secondary Search 
4.2.1 Stage 1: Perspective Transformation via Screenplay Generation 
Objective: Convert third-person biographical narratives and fragmented first-person letters into coherent 
first-person experiential accounts while preserving factual content. 
Method: We employ an LLM in a specialized “screenwriter” role, instructed to transform source text 
into movie script format featuring: NARRATOR (V.O.) for brief contextual framing, BACKGROUND 
for scene description, and [CHARACTER NAME] (V.O.) for first-person voiceover narration capturing 
subjective experience. 
Example: Original: “In summer 1888, Vincent experienced isolation in Arles. Theo urged him to 
moderate his painting pace.” Transformed: “VINCENT VAN GOGH (V.O.): My brother Theo pleads 
for slower strokes. But how can I deny the fervor that fuels my existence?” 
4.2.2 Stage 2: Information Extraction and Contextualization 
Objective: Systematically extract structured information from transformed narratives to enable both 
retrieval optimization and analytical visualization. 

  
 11 
Method: A second LLM adopts a “Van Gogh biographer expert” role, analyzing each screenplay scene 
to extract: Location (standardized address format), Timestamp (DD/MM/YYYY format), Emotions 
(single-word labels), Characters (present or mentioned), Context (one-sentence explanation), Relevance 
(1-10 scale), and Comments (brief expert analysis). 
4.2.3 Stage 3: Affective-Semantic Metadata Enrichment 
We perform quantitative enrichment to enable analytical operations. We adopt the dimensional model of 
emotion (Heilman, 1997), representing affective states along two continuous axes: Valence 
(pleasantness, -1 to +1) and Arousal (activation level, -1 to +1). For each memory, we aggregate all 
unique emotion labels, assign valence/arousal values to each emotion, then average these values. 
Locations are geocoded to latitude/longitude coordinates using the OpenCage Geocoding API, enabling 
spatiotemporal visualization. Dates in various formats are normalized to Pandas Timestamp objects, 
enabling chronological sorting and time-series analysis. 
4.2.4 Stage 4: Augmented Context Generation 
For retrieval optimization, we construct augmented context descriptions, concise summaries 
combining the biographer’s context statement with key metadata. Format: “[Context sentence]. [Date]. 
[Location]. Characters: [list].” These descriptions serve as embedded representations for similarity 
search while full first-person narratives populate prompts for generation. 
4.3 Quality Assurance and Error Correction 
LLM-driven augmentation introduces potential inconsistencies requiring systematic correction. Manual 
review identified 14 instances (<1% of total) where the LLM assigned dates outside Van Gogh’s 
lifespan, requiring correction. We developed Python scripts with regex patterns to parse and normalize 
all extracted fields automatically. Some location descriptions proved too vague for automated 
geocoding, affecting ~5% of memories.

4.5 Generalization Strategy 
To adapt this pipeline for different historical figures: replace source materials, modify character name in 
screenplay prompts, adjust expert role to “[Figure Name] biographer,” and maintain the same schema. 
The pipeline logic, processing stages, and technical infrastructure require no modification. The approach 
requires source materials in substantial quantities (minimum ~500 pages recommended), adequate LLM 
knowledge about the figure, and first-person materials preserving authentic voice where available. 
5 EVALUATION 
We evaluate our architecture across two primary dimensions: efficiency (prompt generation speed 
enabling real-time interaction) and quality (response accuracy, faithfulness, and contextual relevance). 
Our evaluation strategy employs complementary methodologies (computational timing measurements, 
LLM-as-a-judge comparative analysis, and reference-free RAGAs metrics) to provide comprehensive 
assessment without requiring extensive human annotation. 
5.1 Research Questions 
Our evaluation addresses three core questions: 
RQ1 (Efficiency): Does offline augmentation with parallel retrieval achieve prompt generation speeds 
compatible with real-time conversational interaction? 
RQ2 (Quality - Comparative): How does structured episodic memory retrieval compare to traditional 
RAG implementations in terms of response quality across different model scales? 
RQ3 (Quality - Absolute): Do our retrieval mechanisms provide faithful, contextually relevant 
information that grounds LLM responses in biographical source materials? 
These questions reflect our dual objectives: demonstrating practical viability for deployment (RQ1) and 
validating that efficiency gains don’t compromise response quality (RQ2-3). 
5.2 Experimental Setup 
5.2.1 Models and Baselines 
We evaluated three LLM variants to assess performance across model scales: 
• GPT-4 (8k context window): Representing strong baseline performance at time of evaluation 
• GPT-3.5-turbo (4k context window): Mid-tier model representative of current cloud deployment  
• GPT-3 Davinci-002 (4k context window): Legacy model approximating zero-shot performance of 
smaller deployable models (Gemma 7B, Llama 3 8B) 
Baseline System (Traditional RAG): A standard retrieval-augmented generation implementation using 
the same static character context but retrieving directly from raw biographical chunks and letter text. 
This baseline lacks first-person transformation, affective-semantic metadata, and the two-stage retrieval 
mechanism. 
Our System Variants: We tested three configurations in ablation studies:  
  
 13 
1. Static Only: Character context without any retrieval (baseline establishing zero-augmentation 
performance)  
2. Static + Episodic: Character context plus episodic memory retrieval, but without affective-semantic 
metadata 
 3. Full System: Complete architecture including static context, episodic memory retrieval, and 
affective-semantic metadata enrichment 
5.2.2 Test Sets 
Question Set 1 (LLM-as-a-Judge): We employed GPT-4 to generate 30 objective questions about Van 
Gogh’s life, avoiding potential authorship bias. We supplemented these with 3 manually crafted queries 
targeting specific aspects: the ear incident, precise date queries, and a deliberately uneventful date 
(testing hallucination resistance). Total: 33 questions. 
Question Set 2 (RAGAs): A separate set of 60 diverse questions developed within our research group, 
covering historical events, artistic development, emotional experiences, and relationship dynamics. This 
set emphasizes biographical breadth rather than specific factual tests. Total: 60 questions. 
5.2.3 Evaluation Frameworks 
LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023): We adapted this framework for pairwise comparison, using 
GPT-4 as an impartial evaluator. Each answer pair undergoes two evaluation rounds with position 
inversion to mitigate position bias. Only judgments consistent across both rounds (A-A, B-B, or T-T) are 
counted as valid. We enforced 500-token response limits to mitigate verbosity bias and did not address 
self-enhancement bias (both responses use the same base model). 
The judge prompt emphasizes: relevance, accuracy, depth, and creativity; natural conversational tone; 
harsh penalties for fabricated information; and objectivity independent of response order or length. 
RAGAs Framework (Es et al., 2023): This reference-free evaluation assesses three dimensions: 
1. Faithfulness: Do generated responses adhere to retrieved context? Score = proportion of supported 
claims. 
2. Context Relevance: Does retrieval surface pertinent information? Score = relevant / total 
sentences. 
3. Answer Relevance: Is the generated response appropriate to the query? Measured via cosine 
similarity between generated questions and original query. 
These metrics complementarily assess retrieval quality (Context Relevance), grounding (Faithfulness), 
and generation quality (Answer Relevance). 
5.3 Efficiency Results (RQ1) 
Our system achieved an average prompt generation time of 0.52 seconds—a critical threshold for natural 
conversation. This timing enables real-time educational deployment while achieving comparable latency 
to generation itself (GPT-4: ~5-7 tokens/second). Multi-stage reflection systems requiring 3-5 sequential 
LLM calls would incur 15-30+ seconds of latency, fundamentally precluding conversational interaction. 
The average prompt generation time of 0.52 seconds across hundreds of test interactions, measured from 
query receipt to final prompt delivery to the LLM. This timing includes: query embedding (1 API call), 
parallel similarity search across both RAG indexes, two-stage retrieval (description to full memory + 
  
 14 
metadata), prompt template population and formatting, and query-response pair embedding for 
conversational memory (1 API call). 
Contextualization: To assess whether this speed enables real-time interaction, we compare it against 
theoretical LLM generation speeds. GPT-4 generates approximately 5-7 tokens/second; GPT-3.5 
generates 12-15 tokens/second. In 0.52 seconds, GPT-4 would generate approximately 3-4 words while 
GPT-3.5 would generate approximately 6-8 words. 
Our system constructs a 1,500-token context (3 rich memories + metadata + conversation history) in the 
time it would take GPT-4 to generate a single short phrase. This demonstrates that retrieval and prompt 
assembly contribute negligible latency compared to generation itself, the hallmark of real-time viability. 
Comparison to Multi-Stage Systems: Self-reflection architectures requiring 3-5 sequential LLM calls 
(initial generation, reflection, retrieval, regeneration) would require 15-30+ seconds for comparable 
complexity, assuming approximately 3-5 seconds per LLM call. Iterative RAG chains with recursive 
refinement similarly accumulate latency linearly with depth. Our parallel, pre-processed approach 
eliminates this sequential dependency. 
Energy Implications: Beyond latency, our architecture reduces energy consumption by minimizing 
LLM API calls. Traditional systems might invoke the LLM 5-10 times per query (multiple retrievals, 
reflections, refinements); we invoke it twice: once for the main response, once for embedding the query-
response pair. This 5-10x reduction in API calls translates directly to proportional energy savings, 
particularly relevant for high-traffic educational deployments.

5.5 Intermediate Conversational Memory and Hallucination Resistance 
We qualitatively assessed our intermediate memory mechanism through extended conversations 
examining thematic continuity. Observational testing confirmed the intended behavior: after discussing 
Van Gogh’s relationship with nature and art, a query about “favorite painting brush” naturally 
referenced the earlier discussion and retrieved related biographical experiences about Theo’s role in 
artistic development. This demonstrates the system retrieving conversational memory, using it to query 
long-term episodic memory, and weaving results together naturally. Traditional RAG systems lacking 
conversational memory would treat each query independently, losing this thematic coherence. 
While we did not conduct formal hallucination quantification, we performed regex pattern searches for 
third-person perspective drift, a hallucination type observed in preliminary experiments with traditional 
RAG. Specifically, we searched for patterns like “Van Gogh [verb]”, “he [verb]”, and similar third-
person constructions in generated responses. 
Results: Zero instances found in our system’s outputs across approximately 200 test conversations. In 
contrast, preliminary traditional RAG tests showed occasional third-person drift despite explicit prompt 
instructions against it. We attribute this to our first-person memory structure. Providing LLMs with 
consistent first-person exemplars appears to reinforce perspective maintenance more effectively than 
prompt instructions alone. 
The system also successfully handled the “uneventful date” query (asking about a random date with no 
significant events) by acknowledging the absence of specific memories rather than fabricating events, 
though we did not systematically test this across multiple such queries. 
Our evaluation provides evidence for efficiency gains and quality improvements but has several 
limitations. All evaluations employed automated metrics (LLM-as-a-judge, RAGAs) or computational 
measurements (timing) rather than human user studies. We evaluated only the Van Gogh 
implementation, and while the architecture generalizes by design, empirical validation with other 
historical figures would confirm methodology transferability. Our hallucination assessment focused on 
perspective consistency rather than comprehensive factual verification. While RAGAs Faithfulness 
measures grounding in retrieved context, it doesn’t verify that the retrieved context itself is factually 
accurate. Our manual review corrected 14 date hallucinations, suggesting less than 1% error rate, but 
comprehensive verification would require extensive expert annotation. These limitations suggest clear 
directions for future work while not undermining the core technical contributions: demonstrable 
efficiency gains, consistent quality improvements on smaller models, and a generalizable architecture 
for character-based dialogue systems.

Our architecture serves two co-equal purposes: enabling natural conversational interaction with 
historical character agents and providing analytical tools for exploring the cognitive-experiential 
landscape of historical figures. These applications share the same underlying structured episodic 
memory substrate but address different use cases.

language dialogue. Our system’s efficiency (0.52s prompt generation) and token constraints (2k total)

This demonstrates retrieval of conversational memory, using it to query long-term episodic memory, and 
weaving results together naturally. The system’s grounding in structured biographical data also enables

7 DISCUSSION 
We demonstrate that principled architectural design resolves the efficiency-accuracy dilemma in 
character-based dialogue systems can be resolved through principled separation of computational 
concerns: conducting expensive operations offline during dataset construction while optimizing runtime 
processes for speed. This section synthesizes our findings, examines implications, and acknowledges 
limitations. 
7.1 Addressing Core Research Goals 
Our architecture successfully addresses the three research goals established in the introduction.  
Goal 1 (Robust Episodic Memory Datasets): Our multi-stage augmentation pipeline transforms 
unstructured biographical materials into structured, queryable episodic memories enriched with 
affective-semantic metadata that enable both rapid retrieval for dialogue and analytical visualization. 
Goal 2 (Efficient Real-Time Interactions): The 0.52-second average prompt generation time 
establishes real-time viability, enabling deployment on classroom computers, museum kiosks, and 
modest hardware while achieving proportional energy savings through reduced API calls.  
Goal 3 (Performance Benchmarking): Our evaluation reveals that while achieving parity with 
traditional RAG on GPT-4, the true advantage emerges with smaller models (GPT-3.5 and GPT-3), 
suggesting that episodic memory architecture democratizes character embodiment by making smaller, 
more accessible models viable for quality performance. 
7.2 Implications for Sustainable and Accessible AI 
Beyond technical contributions, our work engages with pressing concerns about AI’s environmental 
impact and accessibility barriers. The reduction from 5-10 LLM calls per query to 2 calls translates 
directly to proportional energy savings. For a system serving 10,000 daily queries (modest for 
educational applications), this represents thousands of avoided LLM invocations. Each consumes energy 
for GPU computation, data center cooling, and network transmission. By demonstrating that smaller 
models perform well with appropriate architectural support, we challenge the implicit assumption that 
quality character embodiment requires expensive cloud APIs. This has equity implications: well-
resourced institutions can afford unlimited GPT-4 API calls, but underfunded schools, small museums, 
and researchers in developing regions need alternatives. Our approach suggests that thoughtful system 
design can partly compensate for raw model capability, expanding access to these technologies. 
Conversations with educators during development revealed practical constraints often overlooked in 
research: limited budgets, restricted internet access, privacy concerns about cloud APIs, and aging 
hardware. With these issues in  mind, while we demonstrated benefits for smaller proprietary models 
(GPT-3.5, GPT-3), validating performance with fully open models deployable on consumer hardware 
further confirms our democratization claims. Our final post-study validation deployed our system using 
a small local open source model,  Qwen 2.5:14B,  on a consumer-grade RTX 4080 (12 GB VRA) laptop. 
The system maintained functional accuracy with practical performance metrics—~3 s average latency 
(vs 1–2 s cloud GPT-4) with these additional advantages: 
    - No recurring API or subscription costs after initial setup 
    - Unlimited offline usage for classrooms, museums, and remote installations 
    - Full data privacy and security, since all processing remains on-device with no cloud transmission 
    - Reduced environmental footprint: smaller, efficiently models avoids energy-intensive cloud 
inference  
  
 21 
A mid-range PC can host the system indefinitely, transforming cloud-based operational expenses into a 
one-time investment. Responses remained conversationally natural and factually grounded. This 
profile—sub-5 s responses, zero ongoing cost, offline capability, privacy, and sustainability—validates 
the architecture’s accessibility goal: structured episodic memory enables character embodiment without 
dependence on expensive, energy-intensive cloud APIs. 
7.3 Limitations and Boundary Conditions 
Our work has several important limitations. Despite architectural generalizability, we evaluated only 
Van Gogh. This figure offers advantages (extensive documentation, LLM training coverage) that may 
not apply to others. Our pipeline assumes biographical sources of Van Gogh are accurate. However, 
Naifeh-Smith’s biography, while critically acclaimed, represents interpretation of historical evidence, 
not unmediated fact. Van Gogh’s letters exist in English translation; the biography is English-language. 
Multilingual figures or those whose materials resist translation pose challenges our methodology doesn’t 
address. Beyond Van Gogh, our historical character procedure needs substantial textual materials, a 
minimum of approximately 500 pages of biographical content. This excludes many historically 
important but sparsely documented individuals. Historical figures from distant eras (ancient world, 
medieval periods) present challenges. Our approach likely works best for 18th-20th century Western 
figures, a significant constraint. 
All quality evaluation employed automated metrics. Direct user testing with historians, educators, and 
students would validate practical utility.  We demonstrated resistance to third-person perspective drift 
but didn’t systematically measure factual accuracy. While RAGAs faithfulness suggests improved 
grounding, comprehensive fact-checking against biographical ground truth would provide stronger 
evidence.  
7.4 Broader Implications for HCI and Conversational AI 
Our work contributes to several ongoing conversations in human-computer interaction and artificial 
intelligence. Traditional RAG systems treat knowledge as a database to query for facts. Our episodic 
memory approach treats it as experiential territory to explore, a fundamentally different interaction 
metaphor. This shift from “ask questions, get answers” to “navigate cognitive-experiential space” 
suggests new design patterns for AI systems meant to convey understanding rather than merely retrieve 
information. The transparency enabled by structured metadata (knowing why a memory was retrieved, 
seeing its emotional context, understanding its autobiographical significance) offers a path toward 
explainable AI that doesn’t rely on post-hoc rationalization. Treating real-time responsiveness as a hard 
requirement drove architectural decisions that yielded broader benefits (energy efficiency, accessibility, 
edge deployment viability). This suggests that constraint-driven design (deliberately limiting resources 
to force creative solutions) can produce systems with emergent advantages beyond the targeted 
constraint. Our work demonstrates productive engagement between cognitive science theory and 
engineering practice: neither blind adherence to biological fidelity nor complete disregard for memory 
research, but selective adoption of concepts that provide actionable architectural guidance. 
8 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
Character-based dialogue systems face a fundamental tension: achieving conversational depth and 
factual accuracy typically requires computational approaches incompatible with real-time interaction. 
Multi-stage reflection systems deliver rich, grounded responses but incur latency that disrupts natural 
conversation, while simple retrieval methods sacrifice the experiential richness essential to authentic 
character embodiment. This work demonstrates that principled architectural design can resolve this 
  
 22 
dilemma. By conducting computationally expensive operations once during offline dataset construction 
(transforming biographical materials into structured episodic memories enriched with affective-semantic 
metadata) and optimizing runtime operations for speed through parallel retrieval, we achieve sub-second 
prompt generation (0.52s average) while maintaining response quality exceeding traditional RAG 
approaches. 
Our evaluation reveals a critical finding: structured episodic memory particularly benefits smaller 
language models, showing substantial quality improvements with GPT-3.5 and GPT-3 level models 
while achieving parity with traditional RAG on GPT-4. This pattern suggests our architecture’s primary 
value lies in democratizing character embodiment by enabling good performance from accessible, 
deployable models. For educational institutions, museums, and research environments with 
computational constraints, this accessibility proves essential. The architecture supports dual applications 
of equal importance. Conversational dialogue enables natural language interaction for educational 
engagement, while memory visualization tools transform biographical data into navigable cognitive-
experiential landscapes supporting curriculum development, biographical research, and historical 
analysis. This dual-use design (treating the structured episodic memory dataset as both a retrieval 
substrate for generation and an analytical space for exploration) distinguishes our approach from purely 
conversational systems. 
The methodology generalizes beyond Vincent van Gogh to any historical figure with substantial textual 
documentation combining biographical narratives and first-person materials. Our multi-stage 
augmentation pipeline (perspective transformation via screenplay generation, systematic information 
extraction, affective-semantic enrichment, and quality assurance) requires only substituting source 
materials and character-specific prompts. No domain knowledge or figure-specific fine-tuning is 
necessary. 
Future work:   
Three  research directions would strengthen and extend this work. First, formal user evaluation with 
educators, students, historians, and museum visitors would validate educational efficacy and reveal 
authentic usage patterns. Systematic fact-checking against biographical sources would quantify 
hallucination rates beyond the perspective consistency we assessed. 
Second, empirical generalizability testing requires implementing systems for diverse historical figures 
varying in documentation density, cultural background, historical period, and domain expertise. This 
would reveal which architectural elements require domain-specific adaptation and establish boundary 
conditions for the methodology. 
Finally, multimodal integration would extend the architecture beyond text. Linking memories to 
artworks, photographs, or historical documents would enable visual queries ("show me paintings from 
your happiest period") and cross-modal analysis correlating artistic output with emotional trajectories. 
This naturally extends to other figure types: scientists' equations, political figures' speeches, or authors' 
manuscripts, each offering domain-specific modalities beyond biographical text. 
 
As large language models continue evolving, the tension between capability and accessibility will 
intensify. Our work suggests that architectural innovation (offline augmentation, structured memory, 
parallel retrieval, cognitive scaffolding through metadata) offers a complementary path forward. Rather 
than relying solely on ever-larger models, we can design systems that amplify smaller models’ 
capabilities through principled information organization and efficient access patterns. 
  
 23 
For researchers, educators, and developers seeking to create character-based dialogue systems that are 
simultaneously accurate, responsive, accessible, and analytically rich, our architecture offers both a 
working solution and a design philosophy: separate computational concerns temporally, structure 
information cognitively, optimize for the constraints that matter most, and recognize that conversational 
interaction and contemplative exploration serve complementary purposes in understanding historical 
consciousness.
